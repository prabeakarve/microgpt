The Language Model Notebook
A field guide to building small language models on a single laptop.

Part one: what a language model actually does.
A language model is a probability distribution over sequences of tokens. Given the
tokens that came before, it assigns a score to every possible next token. Training
does not teach the model facts in the way a textbook does. It shapes a function so
that the next token it expects matches the token that really came next in the text.
Everything else, the grammar, the tone, the recurring phrases, the way a paragraph
tends to close, is a side effect of that single objective. When people say a model
understands something, they usually mean the distribution it learned happens to be
useful for the task in front of them.

The objective has a name: maximum likelihood. For each position in a sequence we
compute the probability the model gives to the correct token, take the logarithm,
and average the negatives. That average is the cross entropy loss. It is measured in
nats per token. A loss of zero would mean the model is certain and always right,
which never happens on real text. A loss near the logarithm of the vocabulary size
means the model is guessing uniformly. Most of the craft of training is about moving
that number down without lying to yourself about why it went down.

Part two: the tokenizer decides what the model can see.
Before any matrix multiplication there is a decision about what counts as a symbol.
The simplest choice is the byte. Every string becomes a sequence of integers between
zero and two hundred fifty five. Nothing is ever out of vocabulary, no text needs a
special case, and the mapping round-trips exactly. The cost is sequence length: an
English word of five letters becomes five positions, and a character outside the
ASCII range becomes several. Attention is quadratic in sequence length, so bytes are
expensive, but they are honest.

The alternative is a learned subword vocabulary. Start from bytes, count adjacent
pairs, merge the most frequent pair into a new symbol, repeat a few thousand times.
Frequent words collapse into one token, rare words split into pieces that still carry
meaning, and the sequence gets shorter. The trade is that the vocabulary now has to
be shipped with the model, and text that looks identical to a human can tokenize in
surprising ways. Numbers are the classic example: a model may see one hundred as a
single token and one thousand as two, and that asymmetry leaks into arithmetic.

Part three: attention is a lookup that learns its own keys.
A transformer block has two ingredients. The first is attention. Each position
produces a query, a key, and a value by multiplying its vector with three learned
matrices. The score between two positions is the dot product of a query with a key,
scaled by the square root of the head dimension so that the numbers stay in a range
where the exponential function behaves. Scores are masked so that a position cannot
look at the future, then normalized with a softmax, then used to average the values.
The result is a new vector for each position that mixes in information from the
positions it was allowed to see.

The second ingredient is the feed forward network. It expands each vector by a
factor of four, applies a nonlinearity, and projects back down. Attention moves
information between positions; the feed forward network transforms information
within a position. Stack the two, wrap each in a residual connection and a layer
normalization, and repeat. Depth is what turns a bag of local patterns into something
that can track a subject across a paragraph.

Part four: gradients by hand.
Frameworks hide the calculus, and that is fine until something is wrong. Writing the
backward pass yourself makes the failure modes visible. Every operation has a local
derivative. Matrix multiplication transposes its partner. Softmax subtracts its own
weighted average. Layer normalization subtracts the mean of the incoming gradient and
projects out the component along the normalized input. The residual connection simply
adds, which is why deep networks train at all: the gradient has a short path back to
the embedding.

The check that matters is finite differences. Pick a handful of parameters, nudge
each one by a tiny epsilon in both directions, and compare the slope of the loss to
the gradient the code produced. If the relative error is around one part in a million,
the backward pass is right. If it is not, the bug is usually a missing transpose, a
missing division by the batch size, or a mask applied after the softmax instead of
before it.

Part five: optimization is a control problem.
Adam keeps a running average of the gradient and a running average of its square.
Dividing one by the square root of the other gives every parameter its own effective
step size, which is why the method survives badly scaled initialization. Weight decay
is best applied directly to the weights rather than mixed into the gradient, so that
the decay does not get rescaled by the second moment estimate. Warmup exists because
early gradients are large and the second moment estimate is still mostly zeros.
Cosine decay exists because a model near the end of training benefits from smaller
steps.

What you watch is not the training loss. Training loss will fall even when the model
is memorizing. What you watch is the validation loss, measured on text the model has
never seen. When validation loss stops falling while training loss keeps dropping,
the model has started to store the corpus instead of learning its structure. More
data, a smaller model, or stronger regularization are the honest responses. Training
longer is not.

Part six: sampling is a second model.
Generation turns a distribution into text. Greedy decoding always takes the most
likely token and quickly becomes repetitive, because the most likely continuation of
a loop is the loop. Temperature divides the logits before the softmax: below one it
sharpens the distribution, above one it flattens it. Top k keeps only the k most
likely tokens and renormalizes, which removes the long tail of nonsense without
touching the head. Nucleus sampling keeps the smallest set of tokens whose total
probability exceeds a threshold, which adapts the cut to how confident the model is
at that position.

None of these settings change what the model knows. They change how boldly it commits.
A low temperature on a small model produces fluent text that says very little. A high
temperature produces variety that occasionally forgets the subject of the sentence.
The useful range is narrow, and it moves with the amount of training.

Part seven: what a small model can and cannot do.
A model with a few hundred thousand parameters trained on a megabyte of text can
learn spelling, punctuation, the shape of a sentence, and the cadence of a paragraph.
It can continue a familiar passage in a way that sounds right for a few lines. It
cannot reason about arithmetic it has not memorized, it cannot recall a fact that
appeared once, and it cannot follow an instruction that was never in its training
text. Those limits are not a bug in the code. They are the arithmetic of capacity and
data.

The honest framing is that a small model is a laboratory. You can train it in
minutes, break it deliberately, watch a loss curve bend, and see exactly which change
caused which effect. That experience transfers upward. The same attention, the same
objective, the same optimizer, and the same sampling rules appear in models four
orders of magnitude larger. Only the numbers change.

Part eight: a checklist before you trust a result.
First, confirm the loss is computed on held out text. Second, confirm the tokenizer
round-trips a sample of the corpus byte for byte. Third, confirm the gradient check
passes on a small model before trusting a long run. Fourth, confirm the checkpoint
reloads into a model with the same shapes and produces the same logits. Fifth, print
a sample. Numbers can look healthy while the text is garbage, and text can look
plausible while the loss is wrong. Check both.

Reproducibility is a feature. Fix the seed, log the configuration next to the
checkpoint, and record the corpus size. When a run improves, you want to know which
of the five things you changed actually mattered. When a run collapses, you want the
exact numbers to compare against. A training log is a laboratory notebook, and the
model is the experiment.

Closing note.
Everything in this notebook is built from arrays and arithmetic. There is no magic
in a transformer, only a great deal of careful bookkeeping. Once you can write the
forward pass, the backward pass, and the sampler yourself, every paper you read about
language models becomes a set of small decisions you could have made differently.
That is the point of building one from scratch: not to compete with the large models,
but to stop treating them as opaque.
